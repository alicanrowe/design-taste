# Principles: what the client likes and hates

Each rule carries the client's words and a strike count (how many separate times it has come up). Higher counts
are the ones that keep getting missed: check them hardest. Newest critique wins over older wording.

## A. The core of the taste (read these even if you read nothing else)

**A1. Realised, not simple. (6 strikes)** Every round that read as a plain base object with an idea attached was
rejected: boots r1 "these designs all suck and feel unrealised", boots r3 "how simple they are", the Swarm moths
"these three motifs seem unrealised", the jewelry "a bit samey", the Bombyx lotus ring "this feels kinda basic and boring". A design is realised when its idea runs through
the whole object, every member is modelled, and it would hold up next to the best maker in its field.

**A2. The motif is the structure, never a thing stuck on. (7 strikes)** What gets loved: the heel that *is* the
hoop, the foot that is the leg continuing (Durumi's crane foot: "does the best job at being consistent with the
theme"), wings whose longest feather lands on the leg as a brace, a jail grille that is the cast lancet with the
moth hanging from it ("fucking sick"). What gets rejected: a ram horn ring bolted on an ankle ("so stupid and
gimmicky"), a ball on a stalk to pass a tip rule ("this ball is weird", "lumpy spike"), a small floating ring
behind a heel, a bug pictogram pinned at a crossing ("the literal bug sucks"), straight clip bars poking out of a
body, chair legs that "feel separate from the boddies" (stock tubes under a cast seat and back). Test: delete the feature in your head. If what is left is a normal, plain object, the feature was a gimmick.

**A3. One language, head to toe. (5 strikes)** Every part speaks the piece's own language: "this part of carved
doesnt fit the rest of the piece" (smooth chrome hooks on an all-carved piece), "the smooth abdomen ... doesn't
fit", "why do they all have elf shoes" (one stock foot on every variant), "this handle is awful, have you learned
nothing" (a generic handle on a gothic case). New parts are built from motifs already on the piece; list them
before designing. Hero views hide the odd one out: look at a close-up of every part.

**A4. Consistent detail quality. (5 strikes)** "the details are inconsistent in quality", "crazy low poly",
"line weights seem unresolved", "like 2% too messy", "why cant these petals be as detailed as those feathers?"
(Bombyx: the carved LOULAN feathers were "a really cool piece"; the open-outline petals beside them were not). The
best-detailed piece in a set is the bar for every other piece in it. Every part is modelled to the same fidelity and finish. A
richly cast buckle on a default-shell boot, lames on the sleeve of an otherwise blank jacket, or a stitched line
where its neighbours are cast relief all read as unfinished.

**A5. Sculpted surfaces, no flat dead fields. (4 strikes)** "they have a lot of flat surfaces", "the plain band
side reads weak; every face of a ring should carry detail" ("meh from this view"), flat laser-cut plates
"crude" twice ("i dont like try again", "this sucks") until the same design was cast in relief. Surfaces are
modelled: layered in depth, relief, overlap, over-and-under, crimping, moulding, carving. Quiet areas exist, but
they are deliberate rest fields bounded by detail (A9), not big undifferentiated panels. Cardoon v1 (23 Nov,
"can be pushed a lil further"): a bud cut as one flat outline reads as a paddle from the side; build the volume from the parts.

**A6. Solid, never flimsy or floppy. (9 strikes)** "details that are flimsy or floppy", "this spike seems
fragile", "seems fragile" (earring wire), "these gaps would be too weak in real life", "not possible in real
life" (long thin dangling tail), "cant have any floating pieces bro", "is this not too fragile could it support
the weight of a man", "wavy as hell" (bars), "this being the only connection point seems a lil weak" (a stone head carried on one vine
tip). A head or any heavy part is tied in at several points and is seen to be: members flow into it, wrap under it
or root into a seat, never end as a stub touching its side. Details read heavy and stiff: thick roots, tied-in ends, real
section, closed loops. Nothing dangles, droops, flaps, wobbles or floats. Hanging parts are short and
deliberate (the unequal chain drops are the exception that was loved: short, cast as one, weighty stones).

**A7. Dark, gothic, menacing beats pretty. (4 strikes)** "a lot scarier or gothic", "have you learned nothing",
the jail moth hanging like a bat, thorned vines ("love the spiked vines running up the posts"), oxidised metal,
oxblood leather ("perfect just make the leather red"), angelic vs demonic, baroque. Tidy, cute, generic or
decorative-for-its-own-sake ornament fails even when clean. On furniture (23 Nov) the dark mood comes from mass,
proportion and material, not depicted gothic motifs: see A13. Newer word (23 Nov, twice: Nave "less gothic ... so it
isnt so samey", the Vesper switch cover "soo dark and gothic still"): black anodise + gothic arches is no longer a
default. Products and household objects get their own material identity, lighter (clear acrylic, bare bead-blasted
metal, brass) and plain in geometry; dark-gothic only when the brief asks for it.

**A8. Creative and original, never generic or classic. (4 strikes)** Boots r2: "super ugly and unoriginal ...
your understanding of design is somehow getting worse. I want them to be more creative." Benchmarking against
classic types produced classic-looking work. Take function and makeability from classics; take the look from the
brief. Every design needs one silhouette idea you can see in the main view. Plissé v1, 23 Nov: "Plissé is a good name but the project sucks" (a paper-fan of radial folds round a hub, the
first picture anyone has of "pleated"). A name or technique is a starting point, not the design: when the brief is a
technique (pleat, weave, knot), find the maker who owns it (for pleats, Issey Miyake: one sheet becomes the form,
the pleat is the structure) and take the idea, never the stock image or that maker's products.

**A9. One focal point and quiet space. (7 strikes)** "this is kinda busy", "too much going on" (twice), "messy",
"the base of chrysallis is pretty ugly just looks like a black mess" (a foot of 16 thin twisting stepped steel slices: in
near-black, many small steps read as noise; a base is a few large quiet planes with a crisp outline),
"noisy and random", "a mess" (a whole stud family), "this a bit of a mess geometrically" (Bombyx streamers that
crossed and merged at random). Three or four motifs per piece, detail clustered at joins and
terminals, a focal point with breathing room, crossing members passing clean. Busy is not the same as realised:
A1 is about every part being designed, A9 about where the eye lands.

**A10. Sellable at the best store in its field. (standing bar)** "the product should be sellable at Commune ...
run recursive checking until you think this is a product they would make stock". Artisanal, considered, made of
real materials by a real process, hardware as jewelry. Not costume, not cosplay, not a render trick.

**A11. A sculpture that does something. (2)** "I also prefer when the sculptures have like some sort of like
actual function. So maybe like storage devices or like jewelry holders or lighting elements, something more, a bit
more dynamic or multimodal" (23 Nov, Flatwork round 2). A pure object with no use reads as "the dumbest thing ever"
(Oculus, a flat decorative wall roundel). Give every sculpture a real job: it holds, stores, hangs, lights or carries
something, and the job is built from the motif (A2), not a tray or bulb added on. A purely decorative flat wall
disc fails. Self-check: can you say in five words what it is for, and does the use show in the render?
Second strike: "they still seem kinda useless or pointless" (23 Nov, Chord and Writhe, round-1 spires with no job,
still on the site after round 2 got uses). A use added to the new pieces does not excuse the old ones: when the client
asks for function, go back over every live piece in the family. Best fix when merging two pure sculptures: take the way
they were made (plates stacked on a rod) and let the use come from the same process (the plates become trays that swing
on the rod), so the sculpture is the object in use, not a sculpture with a tray added.

**A13. Made, not depicted: restraint over theme. (3: seating, Flatwork Tensor, Flatwork Catena)** "all the caste chairs are a little bit ugly or
campy or kitsche" (23 Nov, the cast chairs: a spine, a cathedral window, claw ribs, a spiked halo, organ pipes, a
leaf, thorns, a coil). An object that *depicts* something (a bone, an arch, a creature, a plant) reads as a themed
prop: campy, costume, kitsch, however well it is modelled. The client pointed at Tom Sachs, Frank Gehry and Dieter
Rams for the fix. Rules that come out of them:
- **One idea per piece, said in five words** ("the back is a standing slab"). Everything else is quiet (Rams:
  less, but better; as little design as possible).
- **The idea comes from how it is made**: a fold, a pour, a lamination, a lean, a bolt, a counterweight. The
  material makes the gesture (Gehry: the cardboard's edge, the bent maple strip), the process is not hidden (Sachs:
  standard stock, visible fasteners, tie holes and seams left honest).
- **Mood comes from mass, proportion, material and colour, never from a depicted motif.** A7's dark, gothic,
  menacing still holds, but as a tall narrow proportion, a heavy black cast, a blade-thin steel edge, oxblood
  pigment, a severe silhouette, not arches, thorns, bones or spikes. Newer word wins: on furniture, a gothic
  *picture* (tracery, lancets, spikes) now fails; a gothic *weight and proportion* passes.
- **Realised (A1) through detail at the joints, not ornament**: every edge, foot, bolt, washer, seam and section is
  considered and thorough (Rams), so a quiet piece still holds up close.
- Test: describe the piece without naming a thing it looks like. If the only description is "a chair shaped like
  a ___", it is kitsch.
- **But restraint is not plainness. (23 Nov, same day: "i hate them all too boring")** The first restrained round
  (a leaning slab chair, a plate between two posts, a folded-plane stool) passed the kitsch test and was rejected
  as boring. Rams is a check on excess, not the look; the look still has to be bold. Take Gehry's *gesture* (a form
  that moves: twists, sweeps, cantilevers, a material pushed to its limit) and Sachs's *personality* (the maker's hand
  and process shown loud), not just their honesty. A piece must be striking in silhouette and worked in surface
  (A1, A5, A7) and still depict nothing. Self-check: would it stop someone scrolling? If it reads as a tidy
  catalogue object, it fails.
- Third strike (23 Nov, Catena v1: "Catena sucks u think u can fix it or does it have to go"): a chain *drawn* in
  flat cut eye-shaped links with rivet dots and note-shaped hooks. The same day Plissé v2 got "plisse is kinda cool
  now": one sheet worked by hand into the form by a real technique, so the flat file and the object are the same
  metal. When a piece is named for a thing (catena = chain), build the process behind it (a hanging net whose
  weight sets the curve), never its picture.
- Second strike (23 Nov, Tensor v1: "it sucks rn"): lancet-pointed blades in a black tensegrity prism. Applies to
  every object, not only seating. Honest is not enough on its own: the process must *make the silhouette* (Plissé v2:
  the fold is the form; Tensor v2: the cable bows the strips into the waist). A plain part tied together honestly
  (a tripod with a cable) is still generic (A8); if the process could be removed without changing the outline, it is
  not yet the idea.

## B. Line, form and finish

**B1. Strokes swell and taper; curves accelerate. (4)** Every element is one continuous stroke: start, swell,
needle end. Tight at the head, long sweep at the tail. No even-gauge tubes ("even tube thickness" is the most
common robotic flag), no kinks ("maybe smooth this curve", "needs smoothing", "geomtry of this curve is weird").

**B2. Smooth, fair tapers; no lumps or blobs. (4)** "fix this being lumpy", "lumpy tumour", "body is mega
fucked", "body is weird". Long spikes and horns taper smoothly; junctions blend without bulges.

**B3. No nubs, no ball ends, no stubs. (10)** "ewwww nub", "bum ass nub", "this nub on the tail is awful",
"nice curve ruined by nub", "this ball is weird", "these nubs could be smoothed so it makes more of a circle shape",
"think these points should be sharper" (Bombyx streamer tips ending in rounded nubs). Every end tapers to a clean point, curls, or ends blunt in its
own section.

**B4. Spikes are sharp and grow from the member; points the hand meets are rounded. (4)** "make this spike sharp and smoothly go into the rest of
the piece as its one piece of metal". Spikes wind along long runs ("spiked vines running up the posts"), never a
straight comb ("these are ugly tho"). Wearables round off points ("prolly too sharp"). So does anything the hand
meets every day (racks, hooks, handles, switch covers): "Jalousie is a lil sharp" (a brass key and letter rack with
knife-point horns and a lancet spine). Keep the taper and the outline, but end every tip in a visible radius
(R3 to R5 on a 3 mm plate), round notches and outer corners (R5 to R20), and break every cut edge about 1 mm.
Sharp points belong on sculpture you look at, not on things you reach into.

**B5. Taut where it should be taut. (2)** "wavy as hell", "eww wavy as hell": bars, frames and straps stay
straight and tensioned. Melting or drooping forms are out ("too dali esque", "awful").

**B6. Real-world fidelity in the render. (6)** "crazy low poly", "is the metal clipping through the glass that
isnt possible", leather that renders like rubber, milky stones, and "how can we up our jewlery collections to reach
this fidelity" (21 Nov, holding up phone photos of a maker's real oxidised, densely carved sterling in low sun).
The bar is a photo of a real, finished piece: dense geometry, real bevels, matte calf, sterling oxidised black in
every recess with the high points rubbed bright, carving deep and dense enough to hold that black, a hard low
light with a cast shadow (never floating), correctly coloured stones. A uniform mirror-chrome silver in a soft
tent fails this even when the design is right. "dont clip them", "always run a
clipping check for everything always man". "their cad jewlery looks alot better than anything uve made" (22 Nov, a maker's
ZBrush signet: hundreds of crisp, round-sectioned swirls laid in layers over a stippled black ground): dense
ornament comes from placing a sculpted element many times over a heavy blank, not from a few swept strokes.
Wood is held to the same bar: "i rekon u can make the wood material and texture a lot more realistic too and polish
up some of those metal elements" (22 Nov, Moth case, before any casting order). Real species figure (ash: open
earlywood pores, straw latewood, rift on legs and posts, cathedral on flat-sawn panels), true end grain only on
ends, an oil sheen not a coat, and metals with polished highs and satin or oxidised recesses, never one flat chrome.

**B8. A contour that reads as one curve is one curve. (1)** "these nubs could be smoothed so it makes more of a
circle shape" (the inner edge of a crescent kinked where it met the horns). When two members together draw an
arc, circle or line, build that contour as one curve and hang the members off it, so the eye never hits a knuckle.

**B9. Ordered rhythm in bundles. (1)** "this a bit of a mess geometrically" (Bombyx ribbons). Strands, ribbons
and vines in a group share one rule: an even fan, a common wave, graded lengths, stacked in depth. They don't
cross at random or merge into each other; a crossing is a single deliberate over-and-under.

**B7. Asymmetry is bold or absent. (3)** "aysmetrical details should be bigger and more obvious so they seem more
purposeful", "asymetrical?" on a jittery wing. Mirror the pair, or make one element clearly, intentionally
different.

## C. Motif and meaning

**C1. Never literal. (6)** "this butterfly motif is far too literal", "the literal bug sucks", "without the
butterfly motif this is kinda cool", "feather antennas are still weird" (feathers used as antennae, 5 strikes).
Creatures are suggested by structure (veins, bones, wing membranes that brace), may be partial or abstract, and
are never clip-art or stencils. On seating no creature, bone, plant or building is suggested at all (A13: the cast
chairs read as "campy or kitsche").

**C2. Motifs read at viewing distance. (3)** Small motifs that can't carry their detail get scaled up into real
members or dropped. Stitch-only lines vanish on a garment; hardware under about 3 mm reads as nothing.

**C3. No borrowed brand signatures. (2)** "a chrome hearts rip off isnt what i am looking for". Crosses,
daggers, fleur-de-lis terminals are out. Never copy a game character's actual garment or a maker's product.

**C4. Each piece has roots and a story. (1)** Collections get researched history, named pieces, and every motif
has a reason. Names are single poetic nouns with an archive code.

## D. Colour and stones

**D1. Light stones, one species per piece. (7)** Deep garnet "a lil dark for my taste", "stones a bit dark",
deep London blue "dont like this stone color" (x3). Use the light end: aquamarine, sky or swiss blue, rhodolite,
malaya, morganite, pale amethyst; warm citrine as a single accent is welcome ("interesting stone choice i like
it"). Same stone on both of a pair. Stones sit on a clear anchor ("odd placement of stone").

**D2. Palette.** Black, washed charcoal, washed indigo, oxblood red, natural tan; bright or oxidised silver.
Leather in black and red when asked for colourways.

**D3. Backgrounds are white unless the client asks for another. (1)** Renders, page images, teasers, contact
sheets, part photos and 3D stages all sit on white. "why are the dancers backgrounds all black they should always
be white unless specififed". A transparent render shown or exported as is turns black in most viewers and every
JPEG, so flatten onto white and run the background check (shop-machine-cad `scripts/check_backgrounds.py`,
website-publishing `scripts/check_image_backgrounds.py`) before anything is shown. A dark background is only for a
piece the client asked to be dark; a dark photo of a real thing (a seller's lifestyle shot) is not a background.

**D4. Overlay labels sit on a flat accent chip with knocked-out type. (1)** Reels and visuals label pieces with
solid colour chips, white (or ink) type in them, tied to the piece by a hairline: "blue with like white ... kind of
like a nod to like OG computing ... I don't hate that". Hairline-only labels read generic and are hard to read over
a busy frame. The accent must suit the materials ("electric blue doesn't necessarily fit like the aesthetic of what
we make"): an inky pigment colour, not a screen colour, and not the same hue as the work's own reds. The studio's
accent and chip rules live in the brand kit's studio theme.

**D5. Reels: every detail gets framed whole and held long enough to read. (1)** Moth reel, 23 Nov: "a few of
your crops are pretty close and cut things off and dont give us time to see or understand the details also we
never get to see the tools or moth monster plush". Frame the whole feature with some air (the full casting, the
whole hinge, the handle on its ridge), hold 2.5 to 4 s on a slow drift, and give every signature element of the
piece its own beat. Fast whips between subjects are fine; fast cuts inside one are not. Movement keeps a hold
alive, so "no dead holds" never means "short shots".

## E. How the client works with a design (process taste)

**E1. When they like it, refine; don't redesign. (2)** Nave, 23 Nov: "I really like what you have right now, but I just want it to be a bigger project with a larger product line ... take the design language and kind of expand upon it": a liked piece grows into a family on its own grid, parts and motifs, never a restyle. "i do really like the designs tho so if we dont have to change
them thatd be ideal"; the tools "based on the old ones ... improvements in the geometry and increasing the
complexity". Keep silhouette, proportion and materials; add real-world detail. "Can be pushed a lil further" (Cardoon,
23 Nov) means the same: keep the name, the idea and the silhouette, and make the idea do the structural work in 3D
through the process (v1's flat thorned outline became a bud built from its own folded brass bracts, the thorns the
flare of the lower rows). Push means deeper, not different.

**E10. A set is presented and checked as a set. (1)** Nine Lives, 23 Nov: "the ninelives has inconsistency across
the pieces bring them to more harmony", "some have low quality graphics or whatever and some have none". When pieces
of one set are made in separate threads or rounds, they drift: one gets a build sheet and the others don't, renders
come out at different resolutions, crops, close-up lists and spec formats, and a fixed line slips (Edge's work top).
Before showing or publishing any piece of a set, put it beside every sibling: same render rig, camera, scale and
resolution, same shot list in the same order, same graphics (3D, drawing, build sheet), same spec format, same
fixed dimensions. A piece without the full kit is not finished. Keep each piece's own design (E1); harmony is the
shared lines and the shared presentation, not sameness of motif.

**E2. A liked change goes everywhere.** "i love these new feet for thread why arent they everywhere keep the
updates eveyrwhere its a rule". Adapt it to each piece's motif. Named exceptions stay (Durumi's own feet).

**E3. A runner-up isn't dead.** "2 is cool but i really like 1 too so keep that in mind when u do the side panels
and jail". Carry the liked option's motif onto the next surfaces of the same piece.

**E4. When the client loves a cast piece, everything that borrows its motif is cast too.** Flat versions were
rejected twice; the cast one was "fucking sick".

**E5. Match the reference until it is one to one.** "train more on the images", "til the CAD looks 1 to 1 to the
images", "recursively improve it til it can produce renders that match syd". Render loops measure against the
reference (colour, light, camera) rather than eyeballing.

**E6. Do what was asked, no more; and be autonomous.** When the client says "no redesign", don't. When they say
"I don't want to check anything", make every call, publish the passing set and report once.

**E7. Physically real.** "recursively check for real world fabricability", "keep in mind what leather i will buy
... so it can be actually produced", "would these wires be purchased?". Every part names its material, stock size
and process.

**E8. Automated production first: laser-cut and SendCutSend parts. (1)** "automated as much possible shold be
the goal for all design stuff laser cut or use send cut send as much as possible you know tjat kinda stuff"
(22 Nov). Design every piece around parts a machine makes from a file: laser/waterjet-cut and bent sheet
(SendCutSend), CNC, 3D print, then bought parts. Hand fabrication and casting only where nothing automated can do
the job. This does not lift A5: flat sheet still may not stand in for a sculpted surface. Make depth from the
sheet itself: stacked and laminated slices that build a carved volume, slotted and interlocking plates, bent
flanges, layered offsets, edge-on profiles. Every cut part ships as a DXF with material, thickness and process
named in the BOM.

**E9. Samples before heavy compute. (1)** "send me some samples to okay before burning a ton of compute on an
animation" (22 Nov, Moth reel). Before any animation or farm render: a few full-size stills of the materials, the
key style frames and the shot list with timings go to the client first; the animation starts only on their OK.

**A12. Near-twins are one project. (2)** "metamer and rachis should be one project they are so similiar", "same
with chrysalis and gill" (23 Nov). Two pieces that share a silhouette idea and a language are shown as one project
with variants (one page, one name), not as rivals. Before a new piece gets its own page, compare it with every live
piece; if it is a twin, make it a variant of the existing project or push it further apart. Second strike: "Writhe and cord
could be one project" (23 Nov): two floor spires of plates stacked on a rod to a brass point. Twins by method count too,
not only by silhouette.

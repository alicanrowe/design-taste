# Shot composition and product rendering, from the @productdesign account (read 25 Nov 2026)

The client asked for this account to be studied for "the types of shot composition and product rendering", every
post. @productdesign is a curation account (about 5,000 followers) that reposts industrial-design work from studios,
brands and students. All 88 posts were read (2019 to Nov 2026, every one a carousel), 594 images in all: 585
stills and the covers of 9 videos. This file is about how products are **shot and rendered**, not about the
products themselves.

How to weigh this file:
- **The client's own critique always wins**, then `principles.md`, then this file. In particular D3 (white
  backgrounds unless asked) and the 24 Nov pick "hero only, keep white squares" stand: one photoreal room image may
  lead a page; every spec square, viewer and drawing stays white. Where a pattern here conflicts, the conflict is
  listed at the bottom.
- It is lower weight than `instagram-saves.md` too: those are the client's own saves; this is one account the
  client pointed at for technique.
- Learn the camera, light and sequence. Never copy a product, a set or a brand's styling, and never name these
  studios or brands in public copy. The post codes in the last section are for our reference only.

## 1. What the account is, in one paragraph
Almost every carousel is a **studio product story told in 5 to 13 frames**: a hero three-quarter on a seamless,
then tight detail crops, then one or two frames of context (a hand, a room, a person), often a process frame (a
sketch sheet, an exploded view, a parts layout, a prototype), and a family or colourway frame. The renders that
read best share four things: one dominant light with a long soft falloff, a seamless that has a tone gradient
rather than a flat fill, real material response (micro-texture, soft bevels, roughness breakup), and a crop that
is brave (the product runs off the frame or fills it). Every image is portrait 4:5 (1080 x 1350, 522 of 594).

## 2. The carousel grammar (the strongest pattern, nearly every post)
The order the account's best posts use. Use it for our IG carousels and as the image order of a product page.
1. **Hero.** Whole object, three-quarter view from slightly above (about 15 to 30 degrees down), product filling
   55 to 70 % of the frame height, on a seamless with a visible soft contact shadow. Sometimes the hero is instead
   a dramatic tight crop on black and the whole object comes second.
2. **Two to four detail crops.** Macro on the one feature that is the idea: a dial, a hinge, a seam, a texture, a
   connector, a knurled ring. Shot so the detail fills the frame and the rest of the object falls off the edges.
   The best are 3 to 5x tighter than the hero, low depth of field, the in-focus band sitting on the detail.
3. **Orthographic or flat-on frame.** Straight front, side or top view, often two views side by side
   (front + profile), centred, on flat light grey. Reads like a drawing; shows proportion honestly.
4. **Scale frame.** A hand holding or using it, or a person wearing or sitting in it, usually cropped (hand
   only, or head and shoulders), in soft daylight. One per post, rarely two.
5. **Context frame.** The object in a real place (kitchen counter, desk, wooden floor, bedside) with warm low
   sun and long shadows, or on a crafted tabletop set. Usually one; the rest stay in studio.
6. **Family / colourway frame.** All variants lined up, fanned, stacked or floating as a group on the same
   seamless; or a grid of repeats seen from above.
7. **Process frame** (in about a third of posts). Marker or pen sketch sheets, a wall of sketches, foam and
   3D-printed prototypes, an exploded view with call-out lines, parts laid flat in a grid (knolling), a CNC or
   moulding shot, CMF swatches.
8. **Closing frame.** The packaging, a case, or the hero again on the alternate background (white post ends on
   black, or the reverse).

The sequence alternates **light and dark frames** in roughly half the strongest posts (studio white hero, black
detail, white ortho, black detail): the contrast keeps a swipe interesting while the object stays the same.

## 3. Camera and lens
- **Three-quarter from slightly above** is the most common hero view.
- **Low hero angle** (camera at or below the object's middle, looking up) for anything that should feel big or
  heroic: cars, vacuums, chairs, the robot, printers. Used with a black or dark gradient ground.
- **Dutch / tilted object, not tilted camera.** The product is rotated 20 to 45 degrees in frame (a pen on a
  diagonal, a power bank leaning, headphones tumbling) so the long axis crosses the frame corner to corner. This
  is one of the most repeated moves; it makes a static object dynamic without a messy set.
- **Floating / levitating.** Objects hover above the seamless with the shadow detached below them, or a group
  tumbles in mid-air (headphone cushions, controllers, colour variants, cosmetics). Common for families.
- **Top-down flat lay** for parts, kits, families and knolling, square to the camera, even light.
- **Straight orthographic front or side** for proportion frames, centred, no perspective drama.
- **Long lens look** (85 to 135 mm equivalent): little perspective distortion, compressed and calm. Wide angles
  appear only in context frames and car shots.
- **Shallow depth of field on details**: f/2.8 to f/4 look; the hero is usually sharp front to back.

## 4. Framing and crop
- **Bleed crops are the signature.** Most detail frames cut the object off on two or three edges:
  a headband filling the frame, the curve of a bottle shoulder, a single dial, a lens barrel. The cropped object
  becomes graphic form, and the viewer reads it as large.
- **Negative space for the hero.** When the whole object is shown it is centred or set on the lower third with
  a lot of seamless above it; it never touches the edges.
- **Graphic halves.** Several frames split the picture in two: two tones of seamless meeting at a line, the
  object on the line; or an object half in light, half in shadow.
- **Repetition and rhythm.** Arrays of the same object (rows of lipsticks, a grid of bottles, a field of
  frames, stacked keycaps) shot top-down or in steep perspective so the repeat becomes a pattern.
- **Pairs.** Two objects side by side or one in front of another (black and white versions, front and back),
  overlapping slightly, same light.
- **Portrait 4:5 always.** Compose for the phone feed: the object's long axis vertical or diagonal.

## 5. Light (the recipes that repeat)
Measured across all 594 images by the background tone at the frame corners: about a third sit on a light seamless
(white or light grey), a sixth on black, about 6 % on mid grey, 3 % on a coloured backdrop, and the rest are
gradient sweeps, sets and photographs. (A rough count by script, not by hand.)
- **L1. Soft-box on light grey seamless.** One large soft source high to one side, a fill card opposite, a soft
  contact shadow, the seamless falling from near white at the bottom to a slightly darker grey at the top or
  far side. The workhorse; it is how most heroes and orthos are lit. Not a flat 255 white: the ground is 235 to
  245 with a gentle gradient, which is what makes it read as a photo and not a cut-out.
- **L2. Black void with a rim.** Black (or near-black) background, a narrow strip light behind or beside the
  object drawing a bright edge along the silhouette, the front almost unlit. For dark and metal objects, cars,
  audio, controllers. The silhouette is the image.
- **L3. Single hard key, long shadow.** A hard directional light (sun or spot) raking low across the object
  and ground, a crisp long shadow as a design element; often with a window or leaf gobo pattern on the wall.
  Used for warm lifestyle frames and for texture (ribbed, knurled, woven).
- **L4. Gradient sweep.** Background that goes dark to light behind the object (top dark, bottom light, or a
  spotlight pool on a grey wall). Gives depth without a set; common for metal and glass.
- **L5. Glow-from-within.** Lamps, toasters, displays and transparent parts lit from inside, the scene
  otherwise dark and warm. The light the product makes is the key light.
- **L6. Soft daylight lifestyle.** Window light, warm, slight haze, real interiors (wood floors, tiles,
  counters), people and hands. Always the minority of a carousel.
- Reflections are **controlled, never random**: long strip reflections running the length of a metal or gloss
  surface, one or two highlights only, no HDRI clutter in chrome.

## 6. Backgrounds and colour
- **Light grey and off-white seamless** dominate the studio frames. Warm off-white (cream) and cool grey
  (blue-grey) are both used, chosen to sit with the product colour.
- **Tone-on-tone colour backdrops** are a recurring device for colourful products: the seamless is a paler
  tint of the product's own colour (sage product on sage paper, lilac on lilac, blush cosmetics on blush, a
  pastel gradient behind a pastel family). The product and ground read as one palette.
- **Black** is for drama, detail frames and dark products; rarely for a whole post.
- **Colour blocking**: two flat colours meeting behind or under the object (a blue plane and an orange plane),
  for playful products.
- Props are few and on-palette: a book, a cup, a plant shadow, a phone for scale.

## 7. Materials and how they are rendered (what makes them read as real)
- **Micro-texture on every surface**: bead-blasted plastic, fine knurls, woven fabric, sand-cast and
  terrazzo-like speckle, brushed lines on aluminium. Nothing is perfectly smooth; the texture shows in the
  highlight.
- **Soft, consistent bevels and fillets** catching a thin line of light on every edge.
- **Translucent and frosted materials** are a strong thread (smoked and tinted clear plastic showing the
  inside, frosted stacked jars, a jelly-like transparent controller, glowing frosted toaster walls). They are
  lit from behind or within so the inner parts glow and soften.
- **Two-material contrast**: matte body plus one polished or bright part (a chrome knob on matte clay-coloured
  plastic, an orange cable on a white charger, a gold mesh on black). The bright part is the detail frame.
- **One accent colour per product**, usually on the functional part (button, cable, dial, pull tab).
- **Honest colour**: whole-body single colours (sage, terracotta, lilac, ink blue) rendered matte with a gentle
  sheen, never glossy candy.

## 8. Process and storytelling frames
- Marker and pen sketch sheets laid in the carousel next to the finished render (sometimes the render placed on
  top of the sketch sheet).
- Exploded views stacked vertically on black, with thin call-out lines and small grey labels.
- Knolling: every part laid flat in a tidy grid, top-down, even light.
- Prototypes: foam, grey 3D prints, white SLA parts in hand, a workbench, a CNC machining the part.
- Colour and material swatch cards fanned next to the product.
- Thin technical overlays on the render: dimension lines, small type, a grid underlay.

## 9. Text on the image
Rare and quiet when present: small sans-serif labels, thin rules, call-outs on exploded views, a model name in
small caps in a corner. Never a big headline over the product.

## 10. How to use this in our work
For any product page, IG carousel or render set, plan the frames before rendering. Default plan (D3 white, studio
accent #1D847E for chips and call-outs, `photoreal.py` for the one hero room frame):
1. Hero: three-quarter from slightly above, long lens, L1 light on a white seamless with a gentle 235 to 250
   gradient and a real soft contact shadow, object 55 to 70 % of frame height, 4:5.
2. Two or three bleed-crop details on the piece's one idea (the joint, the motif, the hardware), shallow depth of
   field, strip reflections controlled.
3. One orthographic front + profile pair on white, centred (this is also the dimension drawing's base).
4. One scale frame: a hand or a body with the piece, cropped, soft light.
5. One process frame from our own work: the CAD, the cut sheet, the exploded view with green call-out chips, or
   the knolled parts.
6. Family frame for any set: every variant on the same seamless, lined up or fanned, same light.
7. Optional single room hero (24 Nov pick) using `hardwood-furniture-cad` `photoreal.py`.
Check the set against this list: does each frame do a different job; is there at least one bleed crop; is the light
one dominant source with falloff; is the ground a gradient not a flat cut-out; does every surface carry texture.

## Conflicts with the client's own rules (the rule wins)
- **Black voids and black-detail frames** (L2, about a sixth of the images). D3 white stands. Take the rim-light
  idea onto white: a strip light behind the piece still draws the silhouette edge on a light seamless.
- **Coloured and tone-on-tone backdrops.** D3 white stands unless the client asks. The tone-on-tone idea can live
  in the green chips and call-outs, not the ground.
- **Staged lifestyle rooms.** Product photography on the site is studio white (critique 24 Nov, Shrine bench);
  one photoreal room hero per page is the only exception.
- **Candy, pastel, gadget colour families** are this account's taste for consumer electronics, not the client's
  (A7 dark beats pretty, D2 palette). Take the shot, not the palette.
